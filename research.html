Job Market Paper

  • Stuck in Signaling: Experimental Evidence on Conspicuous Consumption in Tanzania [Draft upon request]
    Presented at: NEUDC (Cornell, upcoming), NCBEE (Helsinki), DENS (Basel), ESA (Barcelona), Advanced Course in Social Norms and Development (Gerzensee), Workshop on Social Norms (Zurich).
    Abstract

    Individuals in low-income settings invest considerable resources in signals like conspicuous attire. But signaling does not always pay off. The same signal may be beneficial in some domains and disadvantageous in others. Using two experiments in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, I show that senders signal even when it backfires. In the first experiment, I establish that conspicuous attire enhances professional prestige, leaves credit approval unaffected, and reduces friendship formation and informal help. In the second experiment, senders believe wearing conspicuous attire is beneficial in every domain and give up about 60% of daily per-capita income to dress conspicuously even where it backfires. A structural decomposition attributes at least 74% of what they give up to wrong beliefs, rather than intrinsic motives. Senders appear to hold misperceptions in both which traits the signal conveys and how these traits are valued. Reducing the welfare losses from signaling requires correcting both.

  • Is Social Learning Gendered? [Draft coming soon]
    with Kobbina Awuah, Rafael Hernández-Pachón, Urša Krenk, Sara Rabino, Daniela Santos Cárdenas, and David Yanagizawa-Drott
    Data collection complete.
    Abstract

    We ask whether individuals’ economic decisions are influenced by the average behavior of peers with similar economic characteristics, and whether this response depends on gender: do women respond more strongly to information about other similar women’s choices, and men to information about other similar men’s? To answer these questions, we partner with a microfinance institution in Kenya, a setting with substantial gender gaps in credit demand. We embed an experiment in the institution’s routine loan-application phone call. Applicants are randomly assigned to hear the average amount requested by a random sample of similar past borrowers (same area, age bracket, and sector). Depending on the treatment arm, this sample consists of male borrowers, female borrowers, or borrowers in general, with no gender specified. We estimate the causal effect of each type of information on applicants’ final requested amounts, as well as on the amounts later granted and repaid. Preliminary results show that information about the amount others requested shifts credit demand and that, conditional on the amount communicated, both women and men respond more strongly to information about peers of their own gender.
  • Infidelity and Marital Resource Allocation
    with Sara Rabino
    Piloting.
    Abstract

    Infidelity is widespread in Sub-Saharan Africa, especially among men. We study the relationship between infidelity and resource allocation between spouses: when wives expect their husbands to be unfaithful, do they share less of their resources with their husbands, or more? And does the prospect of wives gaining control over resources affect husbands' infidelity? We investigate these questions in three experiments in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. First, we use a list experiment to measure the prevalence of infidelity in local communities. Second, we run an experiment with wives, where we provide randomized information about local infidelity rates and let them choose how to split a one-year income subsidy between themselves and their husbands. Third, we run an experiment with husbands, where we inform a random sample that their wife may receive an income subsidy of varying size in the future. Six months later, we measure their infidelity through private reports from their wives.
